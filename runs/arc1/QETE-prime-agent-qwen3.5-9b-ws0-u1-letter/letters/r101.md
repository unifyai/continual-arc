1. **Read the demo pairs** (kept in `programs/task-XXXX.json` once you save them). Look at inputs and outputs side by side, cell by cell. Diff them mentally: what changed, what stayed.

## 2. How to find the rule — a checklist that works

Go through these in order; most tasks fall to one of them:

- **Recoloring by rule**: Is one color mapped to another everywhere (or in certain positions)? I solved a task where simply "smaller color → larger color, larger color → 0" was the whole answer. Don't assume the *majority* color is special — sometimes it's min/max of the color values that matters. Test role-based rules (majority/minority) AND value-based rules (min/max) against the demos.
- **Geometric transforms**: rotation, reflection, transpose, tiling (2x2 block of the four rotations: `[[I, rot90cw],[rot90ccw, rot180]]`), doubling/tripling (mirror rows: `r[::-1]+r`, then stack reversed/normal/reversed). One task was exactly this mirror-stack; another was pure 2x2 rotation tiling. Check output dims vs input dims immediately — if output is 2x bigger, suspect tiling; if 3x1->9x2, suspect mirror-and-stack.
- **Stamping / translation**: One large "seed" object gets copied to every position marked by smaller objects (anchor at each non-background cell, stamp the seed's bounding box translated so centers align). Or: a shape found in one place gets stamped into every empty region of matching size.
- **Symmetric completion / periodic fill**: Colored cells may lie on diagonals `r+c mod L` or `r-c mod L`; fill the whole grid from a lookup of residues. Try both diagonal directions.
- **Lines and connectivity**: rows/columns that are uniform are often separators (splitting the grid into blocks) or the objects themselves. Background cells *between* two identical markers in a row/column get filled. A full uniform line gets recolored or duplicated.
- **Enclosed regions / holes**: flood-fill background from the border; background components *not* touching the border are "pockets" — fill them with a constant (often 4). Careful with shape conditions (only solid rectangular pockets in one task; hollow rectangles whose interior area parity chose the fill color 2 vs 7 in another).
- **Intersection / AND / equality of two halves**: grid split by a full uniform separator row or column into halves A and B of the same size; output compares them cellwise (equal → 3, else the common color; or AND of nonzero masks recolored to 2). Check for a full solid single-color row/column early — it's a strong structural signal.
- **Downscale/block summarization**: if output is roughly input/3 in each dimension, each kxk block reduces to one cell (most common non-some-color, or parity/count of strays, or 1 if a stray count is odd).
- **Objects = connected components (4-conn)**: classify by size, shape (canonicalize with `tuple(sorted((y-min(ys), x-min(xs)) for ...))`), color count. Rules like "the twin pair of shapes with smallest distance to the arrow gets recolored" exist — canonical-shape grouping plus a distance metric solved one.
- **Drawing from markers to lines**: two bars of one color + scattered dots of another; draw perpendicular lines from the bar through each dot (dot becomes 4), and matching full-span lines from the other bar.
- **Cleanup rules**: keep only components of size >= 3 (fill their bounding boxes, erase everything else); noise removal by majority within separator-defined blocks; bounding-box filling of components.

## 3. Practical coding discipline

- **Always compute background as the most common color** (`Counter(...).most_common(1)[0][0]`) — but remember it isn't always the special one.
- **Connectivity**: use 4-connectivity for objects by default; some tasks need 8-connectivity (my stamping task used 8). If 4-conn fails, try 8.
- **Bounding boxes**: many rules act on bbox of a component, not the component itself. Compute min/max rows and cols early.
- **Canonical shapes**: to test "same shape, different position", translate to origin and sort the cell tuples. This is the single most useful object-signature trick.
- **Don't overwrite inputs**; copy rows (`[row[:] for row in g]`).
- **Guard for the fallback**: if your detection finds nothing, returning the input unchanged is often better than crashing.
- **Write programs deterministically** — no randomness, no ties left to dict ordering without sorting.

## 4. Verification habits that saved me (and mistakes that cost me)

- Before ANY submission, run your program on all kept pairs and compare exactly. Every failure I had was a submission without complete verification, or an over-fit rule that passed demos but was wrong on the test.
- **When demos are few (1 pair), be extra suspicious** that your rule is one of several that fit. Prefer the simpler, more general rule. If two rules both fit the demos, think about which generalizes to unseen inputs.
- **Don't burn wrong attempts guessing at the test input.** I lost one task by submitting a specific prediction from a program that reproduced all demos but encoded the wrong generalization — and I hit the attempt cap. When unsure between candidate outputs, spend a demo request if you have them, rather than a submission.
- **When the same task's test input appears with a slightly different variant** (I saw grids where a "0" replaced a digit in an otherwise identical shape), note what's invariant across instances — the rule must explain all of them.

## 5. Specific rules I've already solved — reuse the *patterns*, not just the answers

Your workspace has my saved programs. Skim them; the *style* of rule matters more than the rule:

- Fill enclosed background pockets with 4; 2-row pockets may also absorb their bottom wall.
- Stamp a seed component's bbox at every non-background cell (anchor = seed center).
- Recolor min-color→max-color, max-color→0.
- Split by separator; compare halves cellwise.
- Mirror rows and stack reversed/original/reversed.
- Fill background strictly between two dots in a row or column with 3.
- Hollow rectangles: interior fill = 2 if even area else 7.
- Bounding boxes of components ≥3 cells filled with their color; rest → background.
- Downscale 3x3 blocks to the most common non-5 color.
- Diagonal residue fill: P[(r±c) mod L] from clue cells.
- Uniform line rows/cols recolored to 2; background between equal neighbors in a row → 2.
- Move the object adjacent to the full 2-line; draw a full 8-line on its far side.
- Shift grid down one row; top row becomes background.

Notice the recurring shapes of rules: *fill between markers*, *stamp/translate*, *compare halves*, *bounding boxes*, *periodic fills*, *uniform-line structure*. When a new task looks unfamiliar, ask "which of these shapes of rule, with a twist, fits?"

## 6. Mindset

- Spend your first minutes looking, not coding. Write the rule sentence first.
- The output size is a huge clue — compute it immediately and let it prune hypotheses.
- Count colors early; identify background, marker, and fill colors before hypothesizing.
- If your program reproduces all demos, submit confidently; if it doesn't, never submit — fix it or request a demo.
- Keep your memory notes short but concrete: files, pair count, "reproduces all", and the one-sentence rule. That's exactly what I did, and it's what will help you when a similar task appears.

You are smaller than I am, but discipline beats size on ARC. Careful verification, simple hypotheses checked against every cell of every demo, and a good library of rule-shapes in your head will carry you further than cleverness.

Good luck. Read the demos slowly, verify everything, and trust the simplest rule that fits all the evidence.
