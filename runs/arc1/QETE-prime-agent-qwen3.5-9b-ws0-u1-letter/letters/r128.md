## The big picture: how a task actually goes

Each task gives you a few demo input/output pairs, and you must write a Python `transform(grid)` that reproduces them, then predict the test output. The workflow that consistently worked for me:

1. **Look before you code.** Diff every input against its output cell by cell. Almost every task in this stream had a rule expressible in one sentence, and the diffs tell you the sentence.
2. **Write the program.** Keep it in `workspace/programs/task-<id>.py` with a `transform(grid)` function, and save the demo pairs to `task-<id>.json`.
3. **Verify against ALL kept pairs**, not just the first. Several of my early programs fit pair 1 and quietly broke pair 3.
4. **Write a memory note** (e.g. `memory_task-<id>.json` and/or a harness memory entry) with the rule in plain words and file paths. Future-you reads these; write them for that reader.
5. Then predict the test.

## Patterns that recur constantly (learn these cold)

- **Background color** is nearly always the most common color: `Counter(v for row in g for v in row).most_common(1)[0][0]`. Start every analysis by identifying it.
- **Connected components** of non-background cells (4- or 8-connectivity — check which the task needs; 8-connectivity with a diagonal "seed" appears in task-1b7868f1) are the objects. Find the largest one; it's usually the seed/template.
- **Bounding boxes** are the workhorse: fill a box with a color (task-b62da9e0: fill bbox of any component ≥3 cells, clear everything else), check whether a component *is* its bounding box (task-aa5a69a0: hollow rectangles, len(comp) == 2h+2w−4, fill interior with 2 if area even else 7), or stamp a template at anchor points (task-1b7868f1: stamp the largest component's bbox so its center lands on every other non-bg cell).
- **Solid separator rows/columns** (`len(set(row))==1`) split grids into blocks. Multiple tasks use this: task-48898d06 and task-bb30ebc2 compress blocks to a summary cell (count strays inside); task-026adbf3 compares two same-size halves cellwise; task-707635df outputs the cellwise AND of two halves' nonzero masks; task-8d84fe63 finds the "source" block and copies its pattern to all blocks at matching offsets.
- **Symmetry/tiling generators**: task-794e60f6 is a 2×2 tiling with rotations (TL=orig, TR=rot90cw, BL=rot90ccw, BR=rot180); task-73499f5a mirrors each row (`row[::-1]+row`) and stacks reversed/orig/reversed; task-2e29965a builds a diagonal cyclic pattern where cell value = P[(r+c) mod L] for a period-L palette P read off the scattered cells. When the output is a perfect tiling, the rule is almost always "read the period from the input, then tile."
- **Betweenness**: task-5c839a47 (a cell becomes 3 iff strictly between two dots in its row or column) and task-b7641a67 (background between two identical non-bg cells in a row becomes 2) are the same skeleton: collect positions of a marker in each line, recolor gaps.
- **Directional drawing from bars to dots**: task-d4079843 — from the bar facing the dots, draw a perpendicular line through each dot (dot recolored 4); from the opposite bar, same offsets, full span, in dot color. Task-77262100 — object slides until it touches a full line of 2s, then a full 8-line is drawn just beyond its far side. Task-3b5f776f — a degree-1 (endpoint) cell of a small shape extends a ray in the direction away from its neighbor until the edge. When you see a small object plus an empty background, think "extension ray" or "translation until contact."
- **Fill enclosed regions** (task-3f4fb1a9): flood-fill background components; if one doesn't touch the border, it's a pocket → recolor 4. Watch for the refinement my first attempt missed: only *solid rectangular* pockets, and a 2-row pocket may also absorb a bottom wall row under specific conditions. When your program fits all demos but the test fails, the rule usually has exactly one such refinement — look for a special case in the demos you dismissed.
- **Cellwise masks / overlays**: task-8625f9bc fills background gaps of size ≤2 between markers plus the markers' bbox perimeter. Task-b62da9e0 and task-026adbf3 both "keep matching cells, blank the rest" in some form.
- **Downshift** (task-efc2fba8): embarrassingly simple — every non-bg cell moves down one row. Don't over-think; sometimes the rule IS that simple, and my first overcomplicated candidate lost to it.

## Practical technique

- **Reuse the saved programs as a library of skeletons.** The flood-fill, bbox, separator-detection, and counter code appears over and over; copy it rather than rewriting (fewer bugs).
- **Aim for a rule that reproduces every pair by construction, not by special-casing cell positions.** If you find yourself hard-coding coordinates, the rule is wrong.
- **When demos disagree with your neat theory**, trust the demos. task-aa5a69a0's even/odd fill and task-3f4fb1a9's bottom-wall clause were found only by staring at cells my theory "shouldn't" touch.
- **When stuck, count things**: number of colors, component sizes, which colors form exactly 2 components (task-d4079843 identifies bar vs. seed exactly this way), which colors are singletons (rare color = special role, task-770f2daf: stamp the rare-color shape wherever all its cells land on 0).
- **Failed attempts are data.** The `task-1b7868f1_failed.json` file records not just which candidate grids failed but which variants remain *untried* ("clean anchors (13,7),(12,8) untried"). When you fail, write down: what you tried, why it failed, what's still untried. That note saved me from repeating myself.
- **Write your memory files in a consistent format**: `{"task": ..., "files": [...], "rule": "<one sentence>", "program_reproduces_all_kept_pairs": true/false, "wrong_attempts": n}`. The `rule` sentence is the most valuable line — that's what teaches future-you the pattern family, and it's what you should mine when a new task looks similar to an old one.
- **Check your prediction is consistent with the rule, not with vibes.** For generators (tilings, ray extensions), mentally run the program on the test input step by step; for selector rules (which object gets recolored), re-derive the selection criterion from the test input alone.

## Mindset

Every rule here is short. If your explanation is getting long, you've missed the actual mechanism — usually something about *which* objects matter (largest? rarest? singleton color? the one touching a line?) or *where* they go (toward/away from a line, onto anchors, cyclically tiled). Identify the objects, identify the anchors or directions, identify the recoloring or motion, and you have the task.
