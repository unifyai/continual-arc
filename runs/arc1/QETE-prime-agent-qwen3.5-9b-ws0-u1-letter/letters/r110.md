## The core workflow that works

For every new task, follow this loop:

1. **Read the demo pairs carefully.** Don't pattern-match on the first thing you see. Compare input and output cell by cell. What changed? What stayed?
2. **Form a hypothesis in words first**, then write it as a small Python program with a `transform(grid)` function.
3. **Test your program against every kept pair** in your `.json` file before submitting anything. If it fails even one pair, your rule is wrong — go back and look again at that pair specifically. The pair that breaks your rule is the most informative thing you have.
4. **Write a memory file** (`.mem.json`) when you solve or partially solve a task, so future-you can reload the insight cheaply.

I kept a running summary of solved rules; copy that habit. Each entry like: task id, file paths, the rule in one or two sentences, and whether the program reproduces all kept pairs.

## Lessons from tasks I actually played

**Look for the simplest global rule first.** Several tasks were much simpler than they appeared:
- task-2264bd4e: two colors, smaller becomes larger, larger becomes 0. That's it. My "majority/minority role" theory was wrong — check the *values* (min/max), not the frequencies, before inventing role-based rules.
- task-794e60f6: output was just the input tiled as a 2×2 block of rotations `[[I, rot90cw],[rot90ccw, rot180]]`. Check output dimensions first! A 2× size output with same aspect ratio screams "tile/rotate/mirror".
- task-73499f5a: each row became reverse+original, and the row stack became reversed+original+reversed. Symmetry constructions are common. If output H or W is 2× or 3× input, look for mirror-and-append.
- task-2e29965a: diagonal stripes with clues — try `value = P[(r+c) mod L]` and `P[(r-c) mod L]`, where L is the number of distinct nonzero colors.

**Check output dimensions before anything else.** Same size → local recoloring, filling, or counting. Doubled → tiling/mirroring/rotation blocks. 1/3 or 1/9 → downscaling by block voting. This one check eliminates half the hypothesis space instantly.

**Downscaling tasks** (task-67dd046a, task-48898d06, task-bb30ebc2): the grid is divided by full uniform separator rows/columns into blocks; the output is one cell per block. The cell value is usually the most common non-background color in the block, or a count of a "stray" color, or a corner-encoded value. Look at which cells in the block are "different from block background" and how the output encodes them.

**Common recurring rule families I hit:**
- *Fill enclosed background regions* (task-3f4fb1a9): flood-fill background components from the border; those not touching the border are "pockets." Fill pockets with a specific color. Watch for extra wrinkles (my task also absorbed a bottom wall row in some cases — the demos told me).
- *Stamp a seed object at anchor points* (task-1b7868f1): find the largest non-background component, translate its bounding box onto every other non-background cell (usually centered). Beware: I went through several wrong variants (anchor at bbox center vs. component cell, 0s inside bbox included or not). When nearly right but slightly off, the note I left ("clean anchors (13,7),(12,8) and (12,7)+0->1 untried") saved me — small variations of a near-miss rule are exactly what to try next.
- *Objects moving toward a line* (task-77262100): object slides until adjacent to a full line of color 2, then a full line of color 8 is drawn on the object's far side. Handle both orientations (line as row or column) and both sides.
- *Gaps between markers get filled* (task-8625f9bc, task-b7641a67, task-5c839a47): background cells strictly between two markers of the same color along a row or column become a fill color (2 or 3). Variants: gap ≤ 2 only, or any gap, or also the bounding box perimeter of all markers.
- *Cellwise combination of two halves* (task-707635df, task-026adbf3): a full uniform separator row/column splits the grid into A and B; output combines them (AND of nonzero masks recolored, or 3 where A==B else the shared background).
- *Hollow rectangles get interior filled* (task-aa5a69a0): detect rectangle outlines (component whose cell count equals perimeter of its bbox), fill interior with 2 if area even else 7. Parity-dependent fills appear!
- *Find-and-highlight minority color* (task-770f2daf): the least frequent nonzero color forms a shape; stamp it into every all-background region of matching size.
- *Noise removal* (task-b62da9e0): components of size ≥ 3 keep their filled bounding box; everything else becomes background. Or (task-026adbf3's sibling task-8d84fe63): one block has the "real" pattern; tile it everywhere.

## Practical coding advice

- Always compute the background color as `Counter(...).most_common(1)[0][0]`. It's almost always what "empty" means, and 0 is *not* always background (some grids have no 0 at all).
- Write a `components()` helper (4-connectivity BFS/DFS with a `seen` grid) once and reuse it — I used it in a dozen tasks.
- Keep your `transform` deterministic and total: it must return *something* even if the hypothesis is partially wrong. Guard against index errors, empty lists (`max()` on empty sequence crashed an idea of mine), and division by zero.
- Verify with a script: load your `.json` pairs, run `transform`, compare grids exactly. Print which pair and which cells differ — a diff of 3 cells tells you a different story than a diff of 300.
- Use numpy only when convenient (`np.rot90`, `np.block` were perfect for task-794e60f6); plain lists are fine and avoid dependency surprises.

## Strategy under the attempt cap

- **Do not submit until your program reproduces every kept pair.** A submission that fails a demo you already have is a wasted attempt, and attempts are capped (I exhausted one on task-5997d753 — my program fit all demos but my test prediction was wrong anyway; sometimes demos underdetermine the rule, and when two rules both fit the demos, pick the one that is *simpler and more symmetrical*).
- When nearly right, enumerate the small variants systematically: anchor = bbox center vs. cell; include or exclude 0-valued cells; 4-connectivity vs. 8-connectivity; strict inequality vs. ≤; fill color parity. Record tried variants in your failed-note files so you never re-try one.
- Save *failed* attempts too (`task-XXX_failed.json` with a note of what was tried and what remains untried). Future-you cannot remember, only read.
- If a task resists after several attempts, spend the effort on the *next* task instead of grinding a likely-wrong submission. A correct solve elsewhere beats a coin-flip here.

## Reading the grid formats

Your saved files use `grid HxW [ ... ]` notation where each digit-row is a row of the grid. When you load the actual JSON, it's a list of rows of integers. Practice mentally scanning these: look at corners, counts of each color, whether rows/cols are uniform, symmetry about center or diagonal, repeated shapes at different offsets. Before hypothesizing, I'd literally list: colors present with counts; positions of each minority color; whether minority cells form rectangles, lines, Ls, single dots; whether the changed cells in the output form a translate/rotate/scale of some input structure.
