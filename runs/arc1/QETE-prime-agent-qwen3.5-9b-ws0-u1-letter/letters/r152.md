## 1. The harness loop, in brief

For each task you get a small set of input/output demonstration pairs. Write a `transform(grid)` program into `workspace/programs/task-<id>.py` that reproduces every kept demo pair, then submit a prediction for the hidden test input. You can request more demos (costly), and you can submit (wrong submissions count against a cap). When a task ends, save a memory file with the rule and lesson — I did, and so should you; your future self forgets everything not written down.

## 2. The recurring rule archetypes

Almost everything I solved fell into one of these families. When you see a new grid, check these in order:

**Separators and blocks.** Look for rows or columns that are a single uniform color — they split the grid into blocks. Several tasks then reduce to a tiny computation over blocks: count stray pixels per block and output a small grid of 1s/bg (`task-48898d06`), or find marker rows/columns and read colors at their intersections (`task-bb30ebc2`, `task-47ed202e`: rows/cols of one color become all-2). A special case: split into two equal halves by a *centered* separator, compare cell-by-cell (match → 3, else bg) — that was `task-026adbf3` and also the basis of `task-707635df` (2 where both halves have their own dominant color).

**Background detection.** `bg = most common color` is almost always right. Most rules are about background: fill it, clear it, or stamp things onto it.

**Stamps and tiling.** Find the rare color or largest non-background component ("the seed"), then place copies of it: at every offset where its footprint is entirely background (`task-770f2daf`), or centered on every other non-background cell (`task-1b7868f1`). For `task-1b7868f1` note the detail that nearly cost me: stamp the seed's full bounding box *including its internal background cells* — don't just copy colored cells.

**Symmetry completion.** Mirror a row/col pattern to fill gaps, tile the grid with a motif (find the block whose pattern deviates least, propagate it everywhere — `task-8d84fe63`), or build 2n×2n from rotations (TL=input, TR=rot90cw, BL=rot90ccw, BR=rot180 — `task-794e60f6`), or complete a periodic diagonal/stripe pattern whose period you read off the sparse clues (`task-2e29965a`).

**Rare-color geometry.** The least common color is often the "actor." Draw lines through pairs of dots (fill strictly between min and max per row/column with 3 — `task-5c839a47`), extend runs along diagonals (`task-3b5f776f`: same-color runs of length ≥2 extend along both diagonal directions until blocked), or flood-fill from seed shapes (`task-770f2daf` is both stamp and flood-fill).

**Enclosed-region fills.** Compute connected components of the background; regions not touching the border are "enclosed" and get recolored (often to 4). My `task-3f4fb1a9` added a subtle refinement: only fill *solid rectangular* pockets, and a special case recolors the 2-tall pocket's bottom wall too. Look for such special-case hair — they're usually visible in exactly one demo.

**Twin objects and a singleton.** `task-0f6e5a2f`: one special shape (an angle/arrow, unique) picks a winner; among pairs of identical-shape objects, recolor one based on distance to the singleton. Generalize: singleton + classes-of-duplicates is a common selection pattern.

**Motion toward a line.** `task-77262100`: an object slides until it touches a full 2-line, then a full 8-line is drawn just beyond its far side. Works in any orientation — code it generically (rows and columns, both sides), not for one case.

**Shifts.** `task-efc2fba8`: everything shifts down one row. Simple, but I got it wrong before trying it — try pure shifts/reflections early when the output dims equal the input dims and most cells look moved.

**Crop/downsample.** Output smaller than input usually means: majority per 3×3 block (`task-67dd046a`, ignore one dominant color when counting), or extract only certain rows/blocks, or per-cell difference between two halves.

**Line-drawing / rays.** `task-d4079843` and `task-8625f9bc`: from bars/markers, extend lines of the seed color toward dots (the dot cell becomes 4), plus mirrored copies, plus filling small bg gaps (size ≤2) between collinear markers and marking the bounding box. When dots have distinct offsets relative to a bar, the *offsets* are the key data structure.

**Color-only remaps.** If output dims equal input dims and cells look relocated wholesale, first try pure color mapping (`task-2264bd4e`: keep higher color, lower becomes 0) — cheapest possible rule.

## 3. Method that worked

1. **Diff first.** Write out where input and output differ. Is output the same size? Smaller? If same size, count changed cells and their colors — that tells you the fill color and the target region immediately.
2. **Guess the archetype** from the diff before writing code. Say the archetype out loud (in your notes), then code the simplest version.
3. **Verify against every kept demo pair** before submitting. I always ran my program on the demos. Every rejection I suffered came from either skipping this or from the program not reproducing a demo (which means the rule is wrong, not the demos).
4. **Write generically.** Handle both orientations (horizontal/vertical bars, row/col separators), both sides (above/below, left/right), and don't hardcode colors — derive bg, marker, fill from the grid via Counter. Demo inputs vary; a hardcoded rule fails on the next instance. My `task-d4079843` solver iterates over permutations of the two bars and validates constraints — that kind of search-with-validation is more robust than clever geometry.
5. **Save demos as JSON** at the path `programs/task-<id>.json` and the program at `programs/task-<id>.py`, plus a memory note. These are your only persistent state.

## 4. Failures and their lessons

- **task-1b7868f1 (failed 4 variants):** the stamping rule was close but the exact anchoring (clean anchor offsets, one-cell shifts) eluded me. When several near-miss variants all fail, stop guessing variants — go back and re-derive from the demos cell by cell, checking *individual cell diffs*, not whole-grid "close enough." Two wrong submissions that differ in one cell are one wasted submission each.
- **task-5997d753 (unsolved, cap hit):** partial-line coloring with 1s; the hidden test had a configuration my demo-fitted rule didn't cover. Lesson: when the rule has free parameters (here, an offset `d` and a choice of which group receives), don't commit to the parameter that fits demos with the least slack — prefer the parameter that's *structurally canonical* (e.g., 0 offset, symmetric choice) when they tie or nearly tie. If uncertain, request demos early rather than burning submissions.
- **task-5c839a47's lone-dot test:** my rule (fill between two dots) had no defined behavior for a single dot. I tried 8 extensions (identity, cross, row-only, col-only, everything-but-dot, etc.) — all wrong. Lesson: **if your rule is undefined or ambiguous for a degenerate case visible in the test input's own structure (count the dots in the test input!), request demo pairs immediately.** Degenerate inputs are where hidden tests bite.
- Duplicate submissions are wasted — I submitted the identical grid twice under different reasoning (note in my failed file: "sub1==sub3 duplicate"). Keep a list of what you've submitted per task.

## 5. Small practical wisdom

- Colors: fill color 4 and 2 dominate ARC recoloring; 3 appears in "between dots" fills. If a new color appears in output that isn't in input, it's a fixed constant per task — 4 is the most common default.
- When counting components, decide 4-connectivity vs 8-connectivity deliberately; ARC shape objects usually 4-connect, but seed+bbox reasoning sometimes wants 8. Try the other if one fails.
- `Counter(...).most_common(1)[0][0]` for bg; second-most-common or least-common for actor, per the task.
- Keep your code short and self-contained (only stdlib; I used `collections`, `itertools`, occasionally `numpy`). The harness runs `transform(grid)` where grid is a list of lists of ints.
- Between tasks, skim your own memory files: several of my late solves reused an idea I'd written down earlier (bbox fills, separator blocks, twin selection). The memory is the compounding asset — write each note as: task id, files, one-sentence rule, one-sentence lesson.

## 6. A final habit

Before each submission, ask: "Which demo cell would change if my rule were slightly wrong?" Find the demo cell your rule is *least confident* about — usually at the edge of a region or at a degenerate object. Fix or reconsider before spending the submission. Wrong submissions are the scarcest resource in this game; demos are second.

Good luck. The rules are learnable, the families repeat, and careful diffing beats cleverness almost every time.
