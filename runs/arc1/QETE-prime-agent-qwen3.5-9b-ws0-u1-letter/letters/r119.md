## 1. The core workflow

Every task follows the same loop: study the demo pairs, form a precise hypothesis, write `programs/task-<id>.py` with a `transform(grid)` function, save the demo pairs to `programs/task-<id>.json`, and verify your program reproduces every kept pair *before* predicting the test. A hypothesis that fits all demos is usually right; one that fits all demos except one you're tempted to explain away is usually wrong.

Write your programs so they compute the rule from the grid (find background by frequency, find separators by uniform rows/columns, find objects by connectivity) rather than hard-coding coordinates. Hard-coded answers fail on the unseen test even when they match every demo.

## 2. Rule families I met — check these first

Going through my saved programs, the recurring families are:

- **Color remapping, role-based or order-based.** Sometimes smaller color becomes larger and larger becomes 0 (task-2264bd4e); sometimes majority/minority roles matter. **Verify which interpretation the demos support — role-based failed me where min/max worked.**
- **Fill enclosed regions.** Enclosed background pockets become 4 (task-3f4fb1a9), hollow rectangle interiors get 2 if even area / 7 if odd (task-aa5a69a0). Watch for subtle conditions: pockets only fill if rectangular; a 2-row-tall pocket may absorb its bottom wall only under extra conditions.
- **Bbox fill / cleanup.** Fill the bounding box of components of size ≥ 3, clear everything else (task-b62da9e0). Or fill background cells on a marker's bounding box plus small gaps between markers (task-8625f9bc).
- **Line/separator structure.** Uniform single-color rows/columns divide the grid into blocks. Outputs can be per-block counts (task-48898d06), corners-of-blocks extractions (task-bb30ebc2), or cellwise comparisons of two same-size blocks split by a separator — equality becomes 3, else the common color (task-026adbf3). Also: full uniform rows/cols get recolored (task-47ed202e).
- **Between-ness.** A background cell strictly between two dots of the rare color in its row or column becomes 3 (task-5c839a47); between two identical non-bg cells in a row becomes 2 (task-b7641a67).
- **Stamping/copying.** A rare-color shape is stamped at every offset where all its cells are background (task-770f2daf); the largest component's bbox is stamped centered on every other non-bg cell (task-1b7868f1); a marker object slides to touch a special line, then a full line of a new color is drawn on its far side (task-77262100).
- **Symmetry/tiling.** Output = 2×2 block of rotations (task-794e60f6); rows/cols mirrored and stacked in a palindrome pattern (task-73499f5a).
- **Diagonal periodic fill.** Colored cells determine P[(r+c) mod L] or P[(r−c) mod L]; try both key directions (task-2e29965a).
- **Bar-and-dots drawing.** Lines drawn from a bar through dots, mirrored from the other bar (task-d4079843).
- **Downscaling.** 3×3 blocks → one cell each, most common non-noise color (task-67dd046a).
- **Split-and-mask.** Two regions separated by a uniform line; output = cellwise AND of their nonzero masks, recolored (task-707635df).
- **Simple shifts.** Sometimes it's just: shift the pattern down one row (task-efc2fba8). Don't overcomplicate.

## 3. Traps that cost me

1. **Order of colors vs. roles.** Always test "min color → max, max → 0" against "majority/minority" against "bg → X" before submitting. Pick the one all demos support, and prefer the simplest consistent one.
2. **Duplicate sub-answers.** I once submitted a grid that duplicated a previous rejected one — wasted a turn. Keep a note (like my `task-1b7868f1_failed.json`) of every failed prediction and what variation you tried, so you never repeat one. Systematically enumerate variations: off-by-one anchors, which color is the seed, tie-breaking direction.
3. **Tie-breaks and edge conditions.** When several candidates fit demos, add the demos' distinguishing features to the rule (e.g., "pick the pair whose nearer member is closest to the arrow" — task-0f6e5a2f). If two demo objects tie, look for a secondary criterion: size, distance to a special object, position.
4. **Don't trust "most common = background" blindly, but it's usually right.** For special colors use "rarest" or "second most common," and sanity-check with the demos.
5. **Verify before submitting.** Run your transform on the demo inputs and diff against demo outputs, cell by cell. This catches 90% of errors. I still sometimes submitted a prediction computed by hand that disagreed with my own program — always let the program make the prediction.
6. **Attempt caps are real.** If a task resists many attempts (task-5997d753 cost me 8 wrong attempts), step back and re-read the demos for a completely different rule shape, or spend a demo request if allowed rather than burning more wrong submissions.
7. **Count carefully in outputs.** Many rejections are shape mismatches (wrong height/width) or single-row drift. When the output is a downscale or block extraction, recompute dimensions from the separators, not by eye.

## 5. General habits

- Look at what's *rare* and what's *structured*. ARC rules almost always key off the rarest color, the unique uniform line, the unique largest object, or the single asymmetry in an otherwise symmetric grid.
- Check exact-translation twins, singletons, and one-of-a-kind shapes — tasks often pick among shape classes by some relation (distance, size, count).
- If the output is a different size than the input, immediately figure out the size law (double width, triple height, H//3 × W//3, block counts) — the size law is itself a strong clue to the rule.
- Keep a short note file per task with the rule in one sentence and the failed attempts. Future-you is a different model; write for it.

Work methodically, verify everything, and prefer the simplest rule that fits every demo. Good luck.
